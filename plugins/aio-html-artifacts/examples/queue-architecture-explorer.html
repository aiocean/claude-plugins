Three architectures, three different operating bargains.

Option B · recommended

Queue + retry ledger

The managed broker keeps transport. A retry_intent table owned by the payments service records why each message exists, which attempt is outstanding, and what outcome it reached; a sweeper re-drives anything past its deadline.

7 weeks · +1 table, +1 sweeper, +1 reconciliation SLO

Option A · today

Managed queue

Broker-native retries with a dead-letter queue. Recovery intent lives in broker metadata, so the smallest operating surface also holds the least business truth.

3 weeks · no new component

Option C · provisional

Workflow engine

Durable execution: explicit state machines, timers, and replay of a failed run from its own history. The dashed rule marks it provisional — nothing here was measured on a cluster this team operates.

14–18 weeks · +1 platform to run, patch and page for

What the run produced, and what it skipped.

LT-204 recorded p95 recovery of 424 s for the managed queue and 91 s for the queue-plus-ledger across 120 injected broker failures, and skipped the workflow-engine arm for want of a staged cluster.

The dead-letter queue has no consumer attached, so a payment that exhausts five receives stops where nobody reads it.

The vendor publishes 44 s median recovery for the workflow engine, measured on their own cluster and workload.

Recommendation for ADR-042

Build the retry ledger. Keep the managed queue underneath it.

The recovery gap is about where intent is stored rather than how fast the broker redelivers: the ledger closes it because a responder queries outstanding intents directly instead of inferring them from queue depth likely (55-80%).

Write ADR-042 against the ledger, shadow it against the broker for two weeks before it becomes authoritative, and book the LT-204 rerun for a staged workflow-engine cluster in the same quarter.

Where it loses: the ledger buys recovery with a reconciliation loop this team owns for as long as the service lives, and it answers only the intents someone modelled. Option C answers questions nobody modelled in advance, and its replay tooling stays better than anything shipped here. The ledger wins on the delivery limit and the on-call rota, not on capability.

What would show this is wrong

  • Re-run LT-204 against the ledger at 500 injections. If p95 recovery crosses 180 s, the ledger misses the SLO under burst and this recommendation fails.
  • If sweeper maintenance runs above 0.2 FTE for two consecutive months, the operating saving that decided this comparison is gone and option A is the honest answer.
  • If another team already runs the workflow engine as a shared platform, option C's operating burden leaves this rota and it wins on visibility.
  • At 10× volume, if a reproduced benchmark puts operator cost per million recoveries higher for the ledger than for the engine, the growth row resolves against this recommendation.

Assumption that would break the whole comparison: the team stays at six engineers (assumed, unverified).

What I assumed, in the open

I assumed the payments team stays at six engineers for the next two quarters; I did not verify that against the hiring plan. Every operating-burden row rests on it — at nine engineers, option C's platform cost stops being decisive.

The 10× volume row prints no number for either option because none was measured: operator cost per million recoveries has no evidence on either side, and a figure invented to fill the cell would make the table look finished while making it false.

Two options dropped before this comparison, and why

Cron reconciler over the existing tables. Cheapest at 1 week, and it recovers on a fixed cycle rather than on a deadline, so p95 recovery inherits the cron interval: 15 minutes against a 180 s SLO.

Event log with an outbox pattern. Answers the audit question as well as option C, and it spreads the delivery cost across every producing service; the 2 FTE-month limit applies to the whole change, not to the queue team's share of it.
